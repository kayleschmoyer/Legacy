# .impeccable.md — Legacy Sports Design Context

## Design Context

### Users

Fantasy sports players (primarily 25–45) who are league commissioners and active participants in baseball and football leagues. They use the app in the evenings and nights — often while watching live games, checking scores, managing rosters, preparing for live drafts. This is a passion and a ritual, not a utility. The user is competitive, data-obsessive, and has deep loyalty to their league.

**Primary contexts of use**: living room couch during games, pre-draft hype sessions, morning roster checks before the day starts. Always mobile-first, but power features live on web.

**The job they're trying to do**: Know where they stand. Make smart moves. Win their league. Feel like they're inside the game, not just watching it.

### Brand Personality

**Three words**: nostalgic, sporty, professional.

Not retro-for-retro's-sake — the nostalgia is earned. Baseball has 150 years of visual history: scorecards, pennant races, stadium scoreboards, yellowed box scores. The brand draws from that heritage without being a costume.

**Tone**: The confident voice of a seasoned beat reporter who still gets excited every Opening Day. Not flashy, not corporate, not casual. A premium experience for people who take their leagues seriously.

**What it should NOT feel like**: Yahoo Sports — blocky, over-stuffed, no sense of craft. Every screen on Yahoo feels like a grid of content units delivered by committee. Legacy Sports should feel curated and intentional.

### Emotional Goal

**Anticipation and excitement.** The feeling right before the draft picks start — when your team is unmade and full of possibility. Every interaction should carry a faint undercurrent of that energy: the draft board loading, the lineup locking in, the live score ticking up.

Not hype-culture energy. The quieter version: a player carefully marking his scorecard before first pitch.

### Aesthetic Direction

**Dark mode primary.** The "night stadium" concept is correct — stay dark. Users are watching games at night; a white screen would break the mood.

But the current implementation is too mechanical. The dark UI needs editorial texture — the same thoughtfulness a magazine editor applies to layout. Not dense, not sparse. Purposeful.

**Visual metaphors to lean into**:

- Vintage stadium scoreboard (big condensed numbers, clear section hierarchy)
- Sports Illustrated circa 1988 (tight headlines, elegant data tables, graphic moments that breathe)
- Baseball scorecard (information density with ritual structure)

**Visual metaphors to avoid**:

- Generic SaaS dark dashboard
- Neon glow / cyberpunk
- Twitter/social feed aesthetic
- Yahoo Sports blocky card grids

### Design Principles

1. **Editorial discipline** — Headlines earn their size. Not everything is equal weight. A league standings table and a player card are different information; treat them differently. Hierarchy before decoration.

2. **Earned heritage** — Gold, seam red, and deep navy appear at meaningful moments (scoring events, match results, draft picks), not as wallpaper. The palette should feel rare and significant, not everywhere.

3. **Spatial orientation** — Navigation should feel like moving through a stadium: you know where you are, and moving feels physical and intentional. Not teleporting. Page transitions must reinforce spatial position in the app hierarchy.

4. **Data is the product** — Statistics are not content to be tolerated. They're the reason users open the app. Tables, numbers, and stats should feel as designed as a headline — crisp, legible, with appropriate visual weight.

5. **Anti-Yahoo** — Never default to the sports app cliché: blocky image cards, uniform grids, team-color splashes. Every screen should have a clear editorial point of view about what matters most.

### Typography

**Display / Headings**: Big Shoulders Display

- Condensed grotesque made by the Chicago design community
- Athletic, editorial, scoreboard DNA — evokes vintage baseball programs and pennant signage
- Use at large sizes for league names, section headers, player names
- Weight: 600–800 for impact; never 400 for display use

**Body / UI**: Literata

- Developed by TypeTogether, reading-optimized
- Warm, editorial quality — not a tech tool font
- Works at small sizes for stat labels, descriptions, and dense data
- Pairs naturally with Big Shoulders' authority

**Data / Mono**: Keep existing tabular figures for statistics

- All numbers in tables should use `font-variant-numeric: tabular-nums`
- Stats columns must align on the decimal point

**Scale**: Fixed `rem` steps for product UI (app screens, dashboards). Fluid `clamp()` only for landing/marketing pages.

**Rules**:

- Big Shoulders Display is the only display font — do not mix in a secondary display face
- Never set body copy wider than 70ch
- Stat values get weight 700; stat labels get weight 400 at 0.875rem
- Section headings: Big Shoulders Display 600 at ~1.5rem, generous tracking (0.02–0.04em)

### Color Direction

The existing palette is strong. Application rules:

**Backgrounds** (maintain the layering):

- `#0A0F1A` — page canvas
- `#0D1526` — subtle surface (sidebars, secondary panels)
- `#111B2F` — panel surface (cards, drawers)
- `#16223A` — elevated card surface

**Brand accents** (use sparingly — these are the earned moments):

- Heritage gold `#C5A55A` — match results, trophy moments, "you're winning" states
- Seam red `#C41E3A` — critical alerts, losing states, "you need to act" indicators
- Deep navy `#1B3A5C` — primary interactive (buttons, links, selected states)

**Text**:

- `#F8FAFC` primary
- `#9CAAC5` secondary / muted

**Banned patterns**:

- No `border-left`/`border-right` accent stripes wider than `1px` — this is the single most overused admin/dashboard UI pattern; reach for background tints, full borders, or leading icons instead
- No gradient text fills
- No cyan/purple/neon accents (the AI palette)
- Don't tint all neutrals the same — vary chroma very slightly between surface levels

### Motion & Transitions

**The #1 weakness**: navigation feels like teleporting. Fix this with positional transitions.

**Principles**:

- Entering a detail view (league → matchup): slide in from right + slight scale-up
- Going back (matchup → league): slide out to left
- Tab switches: crossfade, never slide (tabs are parallel, not hierarchical)
- Modal / sheet: slide up from bottom on mobile, fade + scale on web
- Data loading: skeleton states that respect the actual layout, not generic spinners

**Timing**:

- Navigation transitions: 280–320ms with ease-out-quart
- Micro-interactions (press, hover): 120–150ms
- Skeleton-to-content: 200ms fade

**Rules**:

- Animate transform and opacity only — never width, height, padding
- No bouncy or elastic easing — smooth deceleration only
- Honor `prefers-reduced-motion`: crossfades only, no movement

### Navigation Philosophy

**The problem**: Overwhelming navigation — too many paths visible at once.

**Direction**: Progressive disclosure. Start with the user's active league context (what matters right now), reveal deeper navigation through deliberate action. Don't show all features up front.

**Wayfinding**: Every screen needs a clear spatial anchor — where am I in the hierarchy? Breadcrumbs on web, back-button labels on mobile, consistent section headers.

### Known Weak Spots to Actively Address

1. **Mechanical feel** → Introduce editorial texture: varied section weights, purposeful whitespace that groups related content, not just padding-everywhere uniformity
2. **Page transitions** → Every route change must have directional meaning; implement route-level transition choreography
3. **Overwhelming navigation** → Collapse secondary nav behind context-appropriate entry points; surface "what matters now" first
